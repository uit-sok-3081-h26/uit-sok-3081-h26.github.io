The Three-Model Challenge

Introductory Econometrics

The economic question

A research team wants to study the relationship between education and hourly wage.

Three economists propose three models.

Model A

\[ WAGE = \beta_1 + \beta_2 EDUC + e \]

Model B

\[ \ln(WAGE) = \beta_1 + \beta_2 EDUC + e \]

Model C

\[ \ln(WAGE) = \beta_1 + \beta_2\ln(EDUC) + e \]

GOAL/TASK:

To explain:

  • how the three models differ,

  • what the three models say, and

  • what evidence you would need before recommending one.

Round 2

Interpret Model A

\[ \widehat{WAGE} = -5.20 + 2.10 EDUC \]

Write one sentence interpreting \(2.10\).



Time: 1 minute

Round 2A — Reveal

A one-year increase in education is associated with an estimated $2.10 increase in hourly wage, on average.

Interpret Model B

\[ \widehat{\ln(WAGE)} = \hat\beta_1 + 0.099 EDUC \]

What does \(0.099\) mean?

A. $0.099 higher wage
B. \(0.099\%\) higher wage
C. approximately \(9.9\%\) higher wage
D. $9.9 additional dollars

Round 2B — Reveal

For a log-linear model,

\[ \ln(y) = \beta_1 + \beta_2x + e \]

a one-unit increase in \(x\) is associated with approximately

\[ 100\beta_2\% \]

change in \(y\).

Therefore,

\[ 100(0.099)=9.9\% \]

One additional year of education is associated with approximately 9.9% higher hourly wage.

Round 3

Is 9.9% exact?

“The model says that one more year of education increases wage by exactly 9.9%.”

Agree or disagree? What word would you change?

Round 3 — Reveal

The simple percentage interpretation is an approximation:

\[ \text{Approximate effect}=100\beta_2 \]

The exact effect of a one-unit increase in \(x\) is

\[ 100\left(e^{\beta_2}-1\right) \]

For \(\beta_2=0.099\):

\[ 100(e^{0.099}-1) \approx 10.4\% \]

For small coefficients, the approximation is often very useful. The point is to know when you are using an approximation.

Round 4

Model C arrives

\[ \widehat{\ln(WAGE)} = 0.80 + 0.75\ln(EDUC) \]

Suppose education rises by 1%. What does Model C predict for wage, approximately?

Do not use the Model B rule.

Round 4 — Reveal

For a log-log model,

\[ \ln(y)=\beta_1+\beta_2\ln(x)+e \]

\(\beta_2\) is an elasticity.

Therefore:

A 1% increase in education is associated with approximately a 0.75% increase in wage.

\[ \boxed{\beta_2=\text{elasticity}} \]

Build your model dictionary

Model Equation Interpretation of \(\beta_2\)
Linear \(y=\beta_1+\beta_2x+e\) 1 unit in \(x\) -> \(\beta_2\) units in \(y\)
Log-linear \(\ln(y)=\beta_1+\beta_2x+e\) 1 unit in \(x\) -> about \(100\beta_2\%\) in \(y\)
Log-log \(\ln(y)=\beta_1+\beta_2\ln(x)+e\) 1% in \(x\) -> \(\beta_2\%\) in \(y\)

Close your notes. Explain the table to your partner without reading it.

Round 5

Can you transfer the idea?

Now leave the wage example.

The textbook’s poultry-demand example reports the following model:

\[ \widehat{\ln(Q)} = 3.2 - 1.121\ln(P) \]

Answer all five:

  1. What does \(-1.121\) mean?
  2. Why is the sign negative?
  3. If price rises by 10%, what happens to quantity approximately?
  4. Is demand elastic or inelastic?
  5. What would \(\beta_2=-1\) mean?

Round 5 — Reveal

  1. A 1% price increase is associated with about a 1.121% fall in quantity demanded.
  2. The negative sign is consistent with an inverse price-quantity relationship.
  3. A 10% price increase implies approximately an 11.21% fall in quantity.
  4. Since \(|{-1.121}|>1\), demand is elastic.
  5. \(\beta_2=-1\) means unit elasticity.

If you can interpret this without memorizing the wage example, you understand the log-log model.

Round 6

Prediction: a new problem appears

Return to the log-linear wage model.

For a worker with 12 years of education, the textbook reports

\[ \widehat{\ln(WAGE)}=2.7819 \]

Can we report $2.7819 as the predicted hourly wage?

If not, what must we do?

Round 6 — Reveal

No. The regression predicts \(\ln(WAGE)\), not \(WAGE\).

A natural first prediction is

\[ \widehat{WAGE}_{natural} = \exp(2.7819) \approx \$16.15 \]

When the dependent variable is logged, prediction on the original scale requires transforming back.

Round 7

The prediction trap

The textbook reports estimated error variance

\[ \hat\sigma^2=0.2349 \]

A corrected prediction is

\[ \widehat{WAGE}_{corrected} = \exp\left(2.7819+\frac{0.2349}{2}\right) \approx \$18.16 \]

Why is the corrected prediction larger than the natural prediction?

Which one does the chapter recommend in this large sample?

Round 7 — Reveal

Simply exponentiating the fitted log value can systematically underpredict the level of \(y\).

The correction offsets that retransformation problem.

For the wage example, the sample is large (\(N=1200\)), so the chapter recommends the corrected predictor.

Do not memorize “$18.16”. Understand why predicting \(y\) from a model for \(\ln(y)\) requires care.

Round 8

Good coefficient, good prediction?

For the same worker with 12 years of education, the chapter reports an approximate 95% prediction interval of

\[ \$6.24 \text{ to } \$41.82 \]

What does this tell you?

Choose the strongest defensible conclusion:

A. Education has no relationship with wage.
B. The regression is useless.
C. Education may explain average wage differences, but individual wage prediction is still very imprecise.
D. The coefficient must be statistically insignificant.

Round 8 — Reveal

C is the defensible conclusion.

A model can estimate an average relationship and still be poor at predicting one individual outcome.

This distinction matters throughout applied econometrics.

Round 9

Can we compare the models using \(R^2\)?

Model Dependent variable used by the regression Reported \(R^2\)
A \(WAGE\) 0.30
B \(\ln(WAGE)\) 0.26
C \(\ln(WAGE)\) 0.28

Can we say:

“Model A is the best because it has the highest \(R^2\).”

Do you agree? Why or why not?

Another question: Can Models B & C be compared using their \(R^2\) values?

Round 9 — Reveal

The ordinary software \(R^2\) always describes variation in the dependent variable actually used in that regression.

For Model A, it describes variation in \(WAGE\).

For Models B and C, it describes variation in \(\ln(WAGE)\).

Therefore, comparing Model A’s ordinary \(R^2\) directly with Models B or C is not an apples-to-apples comparison.

Do not choose between level-\(y\) and log-\(y\) models simply by picking the largest ordinary \(R^2\).

Models B and C can be compared using their ordinary \(R^2\) values because both regressions use the same dependent variable, \(\ln(WAGE)\). But \(R^2\) should still be only one piece of evidence, not the whole model-selection argument.

A better comparison on the wage scale

If the economic goal is to explain or predict \(WAGE\), we can evaluate each model using predictions expressed back on the wage scale.

A useful generalized goodness-of-fit measure is

\[ R^2_{generalized} = \left[\operatorname{corr}(WAGE,\widehat{WAGE})\right]^2 \]

For the textbook log-linear wage example, the chapter reports approximately

\[ R^2_{ordinary}=0.2577, \ \ \& \ \ R^2_{generalized}=0.159 \]

Question:Why are these two numbers different even though they come from the same fitted model?

Generalized \(R^2\) — Reveal

They answer different questions.

  • \(0.2577\) asks: How much of the variation in \(\ln(WAGE)\) is explained by the fitted log-wage regression?
  • \(0.159\) asks: How strongly do predicted wages track actual wages on the original wage scale?

Main lesson: Always ask what scale the goodness-of-fit statistic is evaluating before using it to compare models.

A model should not be selected from \(R^2\) alone. Interpretation, economic plausibility, prediction, and diagnostics still matter.

Round 10

AI audit challenge

An AI assistant gives this answer:

“Model B is clearly the best model. Its coefficient of 0.099 proves that one additional year of education causes wages to rise by exactly 9.9%. Since the coefficient is statistically significant, the model can accurately predict an individual’s wage. Model C is unnecessary because Model B already shows the effect of education.”

Your team has 5 minutes.

Find as many econometric problems as possible. For every problem, explain why it is a problem.

AI audit — Reveal

Possible problems include:

  • “clearly the best”: there is not enough evidence to make that claim.
  • “causes”: the regression alone does not establish causality.
  • “exactly 9.9%”: \(100\beta_2\) is an approximation.
  • statistical significance = accurate individual prediction: false.
  • the wide prediction interval is ignored.
  • Model C is not automatically unnecessary; it represents a different functional relationship.
  • no diagnostics or model-comparison evidence are supplied.

Final Challenge

Advise the research team

Return to the original three models.

Model A

\[ WAGE = \beta_1 + \beta_2 EDUC + e \]

Model B

\[ \ln(WAGE) = \beta_1 + \beta_2 EDUC + e \]

Model C

\[ \ln(WAGE) = \beta_1 + \beta_2\ln(EDUC) + e \]

Your 60-second recommendation

Your group must answer four questions:

  1. Interpretation: What does \(\beta_2\) mean in each model?
  2. Economic meaning: What kind of change does each specification emphasize?
  3. Evidence: What would you examine before selecting a model?
  4. Limitation: What can these regressions not establish by themselves?

You may recommend a model conditionally, but you must justify the recommendation.

Exit Ticket

Do this individually.

A researcher estimates

\[ \widehat{\ln(SALES)}=4.2-0.08PRICE \]

and

\[ \widehat{\ln(SALES)}=5.1-1.30\ln(PRICE) \]

Answer in three sentences:

  1. Interpret \(-0.08\).
  2. Interpret \(-1.30\).
  3. Explain one reason why the two coefficients cannot be interpreted in the same way.

What should you leave with?

You do not need to memorize a long list of formulas.

You should be able to ask:

\[ \boxed{\text{Which variable is logged?}} \]

Then decide whether the coefficient describes:

  • a unit change,
  • a percentage change, or
  • an elasticity.

And when predicting from a log model, remember:

\[ \boxed{\text{prediction on the log scale is not yet prediction on the original scale}} \]
