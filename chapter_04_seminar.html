Chapter 4 Seminar Challenge

Prediction, Goodness-of-Fit, and Modeling Issues

Problem 1: Prediction

For the food-expenditure model, suppose the fitted regression is

\[ \widehat{FOOD}=83.42+10.21INCOME, \]

where FOOD= is weekly food expenditure in dollars and INCOME= is weekly income in hundreds of dollars.


Q1: Suppose a household has weekly income of $2,000, what will be weekly food expenditure?

Solution 1

Substitute \(INCOME=20\):

\[ \widehat{FOOD}=83.42+10.21(20) \]

\[ =\boxed{287.62}. \]

The regression predicts weekly food expenditure of about $288.

Key idea: Prediction means evaluating the fitted regression at a particular value of \(x\).

Problem 2 — Interpolation or extrapolation?

The sample mean income is approximately

\[ \bar X=19.6. \]

You are asked to predict food expenditure for:

  • Household A: \(INCOME=20\)
  • Household B: \(INCOME=60\)


Q2: Which prediction should make you more cautious? Why?

Solution 2

Household B.

The uncertainty of prediction increases as \(x_0\) moves farther from \(\bar X\).

Technically, the forecast variance contains a term involving

\[ (x_0-\bar X)^2. \]

Lesson: Prediction far from the center of the observed \(x\) values is less precise — and prediction outside the observed range is especially risky.

Problem 3 — Point prediction versus interval

For a household with \(INCOME=20\), we found that food-expenditure is approximately

\[ \widehat{FOOD}=\$287.61 \]

and a 95% prediction interval of

\[ [\$104.13,\ \$471.09]. \]

A manager says:

“We know this household will spend about $288.”


Q3: What is misleading about that statement?

Solution 3

The point prediction is not a guarantee.

The wide prediction interval shows substantial uncertainty about an individual household’s expenditure:

\[ \$104.13 \le FOOD_0 \le \$471.09. \]

Income alone cannot explain all household-to-household variation.


Distinction: Point prediction is best single forecast, while prediction interval communicates uncertainty about the future observation.

Part 2 — Where does variation go?

Problem 4: Decompose one observation

For one household:

\[ y_i=350,\qquad \hat y_i=300,\qquad \bar y=250. \]

Calculate:

  1. Total deviation: \(y_i-\bar y\)
  2. Explained deviation: \(\hat y_i-\bar y\)
  3. Residual: \(y_i-\hat y_i\)

Then verify the decomposition.

Solution 4

Total:

\[ 350-250=100. \]

Explained:

\[ 300-250=50. \]

Residual:

\[ 350-300=50. \]

Therefore,

\[ \underbrace{100}_{y_i-\bar y} = \underbrace{50}_{\hat y_i-\bar y} + \underbrace{50}_{e_i}. \]

This observation’s deviation from the mean has an explained and an unexplained part.

Problem 5 — SST, SSR, SSE

Suppose a regression produces

\[ SST=10{,}000,\qquad SSE=3{,}000. \]

Find:

  1. \(SSR\)
  2. \(R^2\)
  3. The percentage of sample variation left unexplained.

Solution 5

Because

\[ SST=SSR+SSE, \]

we have

\[ SSR=10{,}000-3{,}000=7{,}000. \]

Then

\[ R^2=\frac{SSR}{SST} =1-\frac{SSE}{SST} =0.70. \]

So 70% of the sample variation is explained and 30% remains unexplained.

Problem 6 — What does \(R^2\) NOT tell us?

A student says:

“The regression has \(R^2=0.90\), so the model must be economically correct and its predictions must be accurate.”


Q: Identify two problems with this claim.

Solution 6

A high \(R^2\) says that the fitted model explains a large share of the sample variation in \(y\).

It does not, by itself, establish:

  • correct functional form;
  • valid regression assumptions;
  • accurate forecasts;
  • economically sensible coefficients.


Rule: Good fit is useful evidence, but it is not a complete model diagnostic.

Problem 7 — Correlation and \(R^2\)

In a simple regression with an intercept,

\[ corr(x,y)=-0.80. \]

What is \(R^2\)?

And why does \(R^2\) not tell you whether the relationship is positive or negative?

Solution 7

For simple linear regression with an intercept,

\[ R^2=[corr(x,y)]^2. \]

Therefore,

\[ R^2=(-0.80)^2=\boxed{0.64}. \]

Squaring removes the sign.

So \(R^2\) measures strength of linear fit, while the slope/correlation sign tells us direction.

Part 3 — Reporting and scaling

Problem 8: Read a regression report

An analyst reports only

\[ \hat y=12.4+3.8x. \]

Another analyst reports

\[ \hat y= 12.4 + 3.8x \]

\[ (se)\qquad(2.1)\qquad(0.7) \]


Q:Which report is more useful for statistical inference? Why?

Solution 8

The second.

Reporting standard errors alongside coefficient estimates lets the reader quickly construct:

  • \(t\) statistics;
  • confidence intervals;
  • hypothesis tests.

A fitted equation alone describes the estimated relationship but not its sampling uncertainty.

Problem 9 — Change the units

Suppose

\[ WAGE=5+2EDUC, \]

where WAGE is measured in dollars per hour.

Now define

\[ WAGE100=100\times WAGE, \]

so the dependent variable is measured in cents per hour.


Q: What happens to the intercept, slope, residuals, and \(R^2\)?

Solution 9

Multiplying \(y\) by 100 multiplies by 100:

  • intercept;
  • slope;
  • fitted values;
  • residuals;
  • coefficient standard errors.

The new fitted equation is

\[ WAGE100=500+200EDUC. \]

But \(R^2\) is unchanged.

Lesson: Changing units changes numerical scale, not the underlying fit.

Part 4 — Functional-form detective

Problem 10: Match each equation to its interpretation.

A

\[ y=\beta_1+\beta_2x+e \]

B

\[ \ln(y)=\beta_1+\beta_2\ln(x)+e \]

C

\[ \ln(y)=\beta_1+\beta_2x+e \]

D

\[ y=\beta_1+\beta_2\ln(x)+e \]

Which is linear, log-log, log-linear, and linear-log?

Solution 10

Model Functional form
A Linear
B Log-log
C Log-linear
D Linear-log

The transformation determines how the coefficient should be interpreted.

Problem 11 — Interpret four slopes

Suppose \(\hat\beta_2=0.08\).

Give the interpretation in each model:

  1. \(y=\beta_1+\beta_2x+e\)
  2. \(\ln y=\beta_1+\beta_2\ln x+e\)
  3. \(\ln y=\beta_1+\beta_2x+e\)
  4. \(y=\beta_1+\beta_2\ln x+e\)

Solution 11

  1. Linear: one-unit increase in \(x\) → \(0.08\) unit increase in \(y\).

  2. Log-log: 1% increase in \(x\) → approximately 0.08% increase in \(y\).

  3. Log-linear: one-unit increase in \(x\) → approximately 8% increase in \(y\).

  4. Linear-log: 1% increase in \(x\) → approximately

\[ \frac{1}{100} \times 0.08=0.0008 \]

unit increase in \(y\).

Same coefficient. Very different economic meaning.

Problem 12 — Elasticity detective

Which model gives \(\beta_2\) directly as an elasticity?

A. Linear
B. Log-log
C. Log-linear
D. Linear-log

Then explain why “slope” and “elasticity” are not generally the same thing.

Solution 12

Correct answer: B. Log-log

\[ \ln y=\beta_1+\beta_2\ln x+e \]

has

\[ \boxed{\text{elasticity}=\beta_2}. \]

In other functional forms, the slope or elasticity may depend on the values of \(x\) and/or \(y\).

Problem 13 — Choose an economic shape

Suppose expenditure rises with income, but each additional dollar of income produces a smaller increase in expenditure than the previous dollar.

Which feature should the model capture?

A. Constant marginal effect
B. Increasing marginal effect
C. Diminishing marginal effect

What kind of functional form might be more plausible than a straight line?

Solution 13

Correct answer: C. Diminishing marginal effect

A straight line imposes a constant slope.

A nonlinear functional form — for example a linear-log specification in an appropriate application — can represent a relationship whose marginal effect changes with \(x\).

Model choice should start with the economic relationship, not with \(R^2\) alone.

Part 5 — Model competition

Problem 14: Two food models

Two specifications fit the same food-expenditure data:

Model L

\[ FOOD=\beta_1+\beta_2INCOME+e \]

Model LL

\[ FOOD=\beta_1+\beta_2\ln(INCOME)+e. \]

Suppose their goodness-of-fit measures are very similar.

Is that enough to choose Model L? What else would you inspect?

Solution 14

No.

When fit is similar, inspect:

  • economic plausibility of the shape;
  • residual plots;
  • regression assumptions;
  • normality evidence;
  • influential observations;
  • behavior of predictions.

The chapter’s food-expenditure discussion emphasizes that model adequacy requires more than one statistic.

Part 6 — Residual detective

Problem 15: Read the residual plot

Imagine a residual plot with this pattern:

  • residuals mostly negative at low \(x\);
  • mostly positive around middle \(x\);
  • negative again at high \(x\).

What does this suggest?

A. Perfect specification
B. Functional-form problem
C. \(R^2\) must equal zero
D. The slope must be positive

Solution 15

Correct answer: B. Functional-form problem

Residuals should not display a systematic pattern against \(x\).

A curved pattern suggests that a straight-line specification is missing systematic structure.

Residuals are not merely leftovers — they are diagnostic evidence.

Problem 16 — Another residual pattern

Suppose the vertical spread of residuals becomes much larger as income rises.

What does that tell you?

Solution 16

The variance of the errors appears to change with income.

That is evidence against constant error variance.

In the chapter’s food-expenditure application, residual patterns raise this issue for candidate specifications.

The formal treatment of heteroskedasticity comes later, but Chapter 4 teaches you to recognize the warning sign.

Problem 17 — Normality test

For the chapter’s linear food-expenditure model:

\[ JB=0.0633,\qquad p=0.9688. \]

At the 5% level, what do you conclude about the null hypothesis of normally distributed errors?

Solution 17

Because

\[ p=0.9688>0.05, \]

we fail to reject the null hypothesis of normality.

Equivalently, the statistic is far below the 5% \(\chi^2_2\) critical value of approximately 5.99.

Careful language

This does not prove that the errors are normal.

It says the test does not provide evidence against normality.

Problem 18 — Build the Jarque–Bera intuition

A normal distribution has approximately:

\[ Skewness=0,\qquad Kurtosis=3. \]

Which residual distribution should generate the larger Jarque–Bera statistic?

A: skewness \(=0.05\), kurtosis \(=3.02\)

B: skewness \(=1.7\), kurtosis \(=7.0\)

Explain before calculating.

Solution 18

B should produce the larger statistic.

Jarque–Bera increases when:

  • skewness moves away from 0;
  • kurtosis moves away from 3.

So distribution B departs much more strongly from normality.

Part 7 — Influential-observation investigation

Problem 19: High leverage or large residual?

Consider two observations.

Household A: extremely high income, but lies close to the fitted line.

Household B: ordinary income, but food spending lies far above the fitted line.

Which is more likely to have:

  1. high leverage?
  2. a large studentized residual?

Solution 19

Household A: high leverage.

Its \(x\) value is far from the center of the sample.

Household B: large studentized residual.

Its observed \(y\) is unusually far from the fitted value.

Important

Leverage and residual size measure different kinds of unusualness.

Problem 20 — Should we delete it?

The food data contain a household with unusual expenditure that strongly affects the fitted slope.

A student says:

“It is influential, so delete it.”

Is that good econometric practice?

Solution 20

No.

Influence diagnostics are a reason to investigate, not an automatic instruction to delete.

Ask:

  • Is the observation recorded incorrectly?
  • Is it genuinely part of the population?
  • Why is it unusual?
  • How sensitive are results to it?

The chapter explicitly cautions against simply throwing unusual observations away.

Problem 21 — Four diagnostic clues

Match each diagnostic to what it mainly flags.

  1. Leverage
  2. Studentized residual
  3. DFBETAS
  4. DFFITS

A. Unusual \(x\) location
B. Unusually large residual
C. Strong effect on a coefficient estimate
D. Strong effect on its own fitted value

Solution 21

  1. Leverage → A
  2. Studentized residual → B
  3. DFBETAS → C
  4. DFFITS → D

Together they help identify observations deserving closer examination.

Part 8 — Log-model prediction challenge

Problem 22

Suppose

\[ \widehat{\ln(y)}=2+0.03x. \]

For \(x=10\),

\[ \widehat{\ln(y)}=2.3. \]

A student immediately reports

\[ \hat y=e^{2.3}. \]

What issue should you remember when moving predictions back from log scale to the original \(y\) scale?

Solution 22

Exponentiating the fitted log value gives a natural transformation back to the original scale, but prediction of the conditional mean of \(y\) requires care because

\[ E(e^e)\neq e^{E(e)} \]

in general.

For a log-normal error, a correction involving the error variance is needed.

Lesson

Prediction after logging the dependent variable is not just coefficient interpretation in reverse.

Part 9 — Model selection meeting

Problem 23: The evidence board

You have two competing models.

Evidence Model A Model B
Fit slightly higher slightly lower
Residual pattern strong curve mostly random
Economic shape questionable plausible
Normality acceptable acceptable
Influential points one important one important

Which pieces of evidence should enter your model-choice discussion?

Do not answer with only “highest \(R^2\).”

Solution 23

All of them matter.

A defensible model choice considers jointly:

  • goodness-of-fit;
  • economic theory/plausibility;
  • residual behavior;
  • regression assumptions;
  • influential observations;
  • intended use, especially prediction.

There is no rule in Chapter 4 saying “choose the largest \(R^2\) and stop.”

Final case — 10-minute team challenge

Your team is given this report:

“Our income model has \(R^2=0.78\). Residuals show a clear curved pattern. The Jarque–Bera p-value is 0.62. One very high-income observation has high leverage and large DFBETAS. We need to predict spending for an income far above anything in the sample.”

Prepare a 60-second recommendation.

Your answer must address:

  1. goodness-of-fit;
  2. functional form;
  3. normality;
  4. influential observations;
  5. extrapolation/prediction risk.

Final case — Debrief

A strong answer should recognize:

Fit: \(R^2=0.78\) indicates substantial sample variation is explained, but does not establish adequacy.

Functional form: the curved residual pattern is a warning that the specification may be wrong.

Normality: \(p=0.62\) provides no evidence against normality.

Influence: investigate the high-leverage observation and assess sensitivity rather than automatically deleting it.

Prediction: forecasting far beyond the observed \(x\) range is especially risky.
